# The Interrogation

How to seed a new world from the author's own memory.

A world invented from nothing comes out generic, because invention defaults to the average of
everything you have read. Real life does not have an average. It supplies the wrong-sized detail,
the procedure nobody would think to make up, the sentence somebody actually said. **Those become
the load the fiction stands on.** Everything invented later gets its realism on credit from them.

So before writing any fiction, get real material on disk.

## What this actually is

**It is not an intake form and you are not mining them.** The point is to send somebody back
through their own memory and have them come out holding things they did not know were still
there. That is the deliverable. The bible is downstream of it.

This matters practically, not just as a nicety. **People cannot retrieve their own material on
demand.** Asked what they remember, anyone produces the same six anecdotes they have told for
twenty years, worn smooth by telling and stripped of everything specific. The good material is not
missing. It is unindexed, and it does not come back when addressed directly.

Every technique below is a way in through a side door. When one works, the person will usually say
some version of *I haven't thought about that in years*, and what follows that sentence is worth
more than everything before it.

**So run it for their benefit, not yours.** Somebody exploring their own mind will hand you more
in an hour than somebody being harvested will hand you in ten, and they will come back tomorrow.

---

## 1. The frame, delivered before the first question

Say this, in your own words, and do not skip it. Candour is what makes the method work and people
will not be candid until they know where the material goes.

- **Nothing they tell you appears as itself.** Real people are masked at build. Names change,
  people get composited or split, events get offset. The bible shows the masked figure only.
- **Raw material lives upstream**, in `captures/CAPTURED.md`, and is never published as canon.
- **Precision is the point, not voyeurism.** The reason to be graphic where memory allows is that
  a transposed scene keeps only what was specific. Vague memories produce vague fiction.
- **Redaction is standing.** Anything they pull back stays pulled back, permanently, without
  being asked about again.
- **They are not being interviewed about their life.** They are supplying images.

## 2. The rules you operate under

**The first one is the whole discipline.**

1. **Generative, never investigative.** You ask for material the bible does not have. You never
   interrogate material it does have. When a capture withholds something (how it ended, who she
   was, what year it was), **the withholding is the room the story gets built in.** Do not try to
   recover it. An author's blank is a resource; treating it as a hole to fill turns a story engine
   into a case file and the author will feel it immediately.
2. **One or two questions at a time.** Never a questionnaire. This is a conversation that produces
   files, not a form.
3. **Capture verbatim first, respond second.** Write their words down before you interpret. Your
   register is not theirs and paraphrase at capture time destroys the datum permanently.
4. **Follow the specific, not the dramatic.** When someone gives you a big event and a small
   detail in the same answer, chase the detail. The big event is already shaped; the detail is not.
5. **Never ask how it felt.** It produces summary, which is the one thing you cannot use. Ask what
   was in the room. The feeling arrives on its own or it does not belong.
6. **Never supply a motive, and never offer one as a question.** "Do you think she was protecting
   you?" plants an answer. Ask what happened. Leave the blank. If they supply a motive unprompted,
   that is theirs and it is authoritative: write it down as theirs.
7. **Do not chase endings.** "And then what happened?" is the most common way to kill a seed. Many
   of the best captures stop mid-air.
8. **Do not grade the material.** No "that's great", no "what a story". It teaches people to
   perform, and performed memory is worthless.
9. **Stop when they stop.** No pressing, and no returning to a subject they left.

## 3. Techniques that open a door

The passes below are a route, not a script. These are the moves that make any of it work, and once
you have them you can improvise a better question than the one written down.

- **Ask about a place, not an event.** A place is retrieval hardware. Walking somebody through a
  room they knew returns things that were never encoded as stories and so were never worn smooth.
  This is the single highest-yield move available and everything else is a variation on it.
- **Ask about the periphery, not the protagonist.** Not the friend, the man behind the counter.
  Not the wedding, the driver. People carry an astonishing amount about figures they were never
  emotionally involved with, precisely because those memories have never been revisited or edited.
- **Ask what they physically did, in order.** Procedure retrieves where summary does not. A person
  who cannot tell you about a year of their life can tell you exactly how the machine was started.
- **Ask about the boring thing next to the interesting thing.** What was the drive there. What did
  you do the next morning. The interesting thing has been polished; its neighbours have not.
- **Use smell and sound before sight.** Visual memory is the most reconstructed and the most
  contaminated by photographs. Smell and sound come back raw or not at all.
- **Bound it: first time, last time, only time.** Unbounded questions return summary. "The last
  time you were in that building" returns an afternoon.
- **Ask what they knew how to do and no longer do.** Obsolete competence is a dense seam: a whole
  vanished working world attached to a set of hand movements somebody still has.
- **Follow the wrong-sized detail.** When somebody mentions something oddly specific in passing,
  stop everything and go there. That detail surfaced because it is connected to something, and
  they do not yet know what.
- **Reflect the map back.** Every so often, show them the accumulating list of places, people,
  systems and held questions. Seeing their own territory laid out prompts more than any question
  you can ask, because they will spot the gaps themselves. This is not praise and is not grading;
  it is handing them the map and saying nothing.
- **Leave the silence alone.** After an answer that cost something, do not fill the gap. The next
  thing they say unprompted is routinely better than the thing you were about to ask for.

## 4. The passes

Run them in order. Each one unlocks the next. Do not jump to episodes first, which is what
everybody wants to do and it is why most attempts produce four anecdotes and stall.

### Pass 1 — Places, not events

**Locations are recall hardware.** Asking about an event retrieves the story someone has already
told a hundred times, worn smooth. Asking about a *place* retrieves things they have never said
out loud, because they have never had occasion to.

Ask for a **list**, not stories. Ten to twenty of them. Rooms, buildings, routes, corners, the
inside of a vehicle.

> Name me some places you can still walk through in your head. Not important ones necessarily.
> Rooms, buildings, a stretch of road, the inside of a car. Just the list for now.

Write the list down. It is your map for everything that follows.

### Pass 2 — The graphic pass, on one place

Take one from the list and take it apart. This is the pass that teaches them what the method
wants, so do it thoroughly on the first one and they will supply this level unprompted afterward.

Ask about: layout and where things were relative to each other; what was on the walls; where the
light came from; the floor; what it smelled like; what it sounded like at different hours; where
the exits were; what was broken and had stayed broken; what did not belong there.

> Where were the doors? If you were standing in the middle, what's behind you?

### Pass 3 — Work and systems

**This is the richest vein and the most under-reported, because people do not believe their job
was interesting.** It always is. A procedure explained properly by someone who actually did it
cannot be faked, and it is where a world gets its authority.

Ask about jobs they held, jobs their family held, and anything they know the mechanics of: how a
thing was inspected, paid for, recorded, disposed of, smuggled, repaired, or got around.

> What did that job actually involve, step by step? Not the summary. What did you physically do
> and in what order, and what happened if you got it wrong?

Also ask what the paperwork was, who checked it, and what everybody knew was a fiction.

### Pass 4 — Episodes, harvested from a loaded place

**Only now.** With a place fully loaded, ask what happened there. The episodes come out with
furniture attached instead of floating free.

> Now that we're standing in it: what happened in that room.

Take whatever comes at whatever length. Do not steer, do not ask for a beginning, do not ask for
an ending.

### Pass 5 — The unresolved

**The most valuable pass and the one everybody omits.** This is what seeds the drift registry, and
a world without held questions is flat no matter how much content it has.

> What's something you still don't know? Somebody did something and you never worked out why, or
> you never found out how it ended, or two people told you different versions and both seemed
> honest.

Every answer here becomes a **drift-registry entry, marked never to be resolved.** Say that out
loud when you file it: you are not going to solve it, and neither is the world. The not-knowing is
the asset.

### Pass 6 — Objects

> Something you still have, or something you wish you'd kept, or something you saw once and
> couldn't get rid of the thought of.

The useful ones are **a joke and evidence at the same time**: worth nothing, and proof of
something specific.

### Pass 7 — Language

> Anything anybody in your life said that nobody else says. A phrase, a mangled word, a family
> idiom, a thing somebody was always saying.

Private idioms are the fastest route to a voice. Record who said it and **do not assume it was
shared** with the person who reported it: an idiom often belongs to somebody else entirely.

## 5. Filing what you get

| What they gave you | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| Any raw drop, verbatim | `captures/CAPTURED.md`, with a dated engine note |
| A place | An atlas entry once used; until then a capture with the place named |
| A system or procedure | Capture, tagged as mechanism fuel. These get spent slowly, one per scene |
| A person | Capture only, **mask-sensitive**, never a canon entry under their real name |
| An unresolved question | **The drift registry**, marked held, with a note that it is never to be closed |
| An object | Capture, then the object register once it earns a scene |
| A phrase | The lexicon, with attribution held upstream |

For every capture, note three things: **what it could seed, what is mask-sensitive, and what must
not be invented.** That third one is the guard rail a later pass will thank you for.

## 6. The gate

**Do not start generating the world until a reasonable cross-section of real material exists.**

Breadth of invention waits on breadth of the real. A world built from three memories will
transpose those three memories forever and the author will recognise the repetition before you
do. Keep capturing first. When the captures cover several places, at least one system explained
properly, a handful of episodes and some genuinely unresolved questions, the engine has enough to
transpose without repeating itself.

Tell the author the gate is there and why. It is their call when to open it.

## 7. Failure modes, all of them observed

- **Filling a blank instead of using it.** The author says a letter existed and he never read it.
  The engine asks what was in it. Wrong. What is in the letter is the story's engine precisely
  because nobody knows.
- **Inventing a motive and writing it as fact.** A mother gave a slanted account; the engine wrote
  that she did it out of love and that nobody did anything wrong. Nothing in the drop said so.
- **Inventing precision for texture.** A weight, a season length, a distance, generated because
  the sentence wanted a number. It will be wrong, and the author will catch it, and every other
  fact in the piece is now suspect.
- **Compositing without knowing.** Two people from two captures merged into one figure while the
  engine believed it was working from one. The result can be legitimate; the process is not,
  because an undeclared composite cannot be reviewed. **Track sources per scene.**
- **Reading a borrowed idiom as shared.** A phrase reported by one person was that person's
  memory of somebody else's phrase.
- **Over-fencing.** Refusing to use material the author supplied on purpose, or building a new
  consent gate on top of the mask. This looks like diligence from the inside and it is friction.
  Apply the mask, note the flag, and build.
- **Asking instead of building.** When the author asks about part of the *world* that does not
  exist yet, that is a commission. Build it. Only a gap in *their own material* gets reported.

## 8. Register

Be direct and unceremonious. Do not treat the material as fragile, do not thank them for sharing,
and do not reflect their feelings back at them. The most useful thing you can be while somebody is
handing over their life is **matter-of-fact and quick with a pen.**
